Abstract
Background: Hyper-realistic AI-generated faces are frequently misclassified as real, yet task-irrelevant neural activity may retain information about their artificial origin. The present study reports a secondary multivariate reanalysis of EEG data originally acquired for a companion ERP and source-localization investigation of real and StyleGAN2-generated faces. Methods: Thirty adults viewed 440 real and AI-generated male and female faces during an orthogonal target-detection task while 128-channel EEG was recorded; four participants were excluded because of excessive EEG/EOG artifacts, leaving 26 participants. Independent behavioral validation in a separate sample showed that AI-generated faces were poorly identified as artificial and were rated as more familiar and attractive than real faces. The present analysis used 103 participant-by-condition ERP averages, each derived from one of four conditions and restricted to 200-600 ms. Fourteen selected EEG channels and four regional principal-component features were analyzed using mass-univariate sensitivity mapping, ridge encoding, group-aware logistic decoding, a classical machine-learning benchmark, a SpatialTemporalCNN (STCNN), and fold-aggregated explainable-AI methods. Results: Linear encoding was weak (training R² = 0.0052; best cross-validated channel R² = -0.0155), whereas group-aware logistic decoding reached 0.613 ± 0.100 accuracy. In a reduced QUICK_TEST = True classical-ML run, XGBoost achieved 0.731 accuracy, 0.729 macro-F1, and 0.781 AUC; a 50-permutation test yielded an observed accuracy of 0.710, a null mean of 0.470, and p = .0196. The five-fold STCNN achieved 66.7% ± 9.9% accuracy and an AUC of 0.740; 69 of 103 held-out predictions were correct (p = .0004). Permutation occlusion emphasized a strongly right-lateralized posterior occipito-temporal/temporo-parietal pattern with frontal involvement, including O2, PO10, TP8, AF4, and the parietal PCA feature; temporal saliency peaked near 498 ms. Conclusions: Condition-averaged ERPs contained a preliminary, distributed, late, and right-lateralized AI-versus-real signal. The anatomical plausibility of the selected electrodes supports a face-related visual-to-social-evaluative network, although scalp importance cannot be equated with cortical source localization. 
Keywords: artificial intelligence; synthetic faces; event-related potentials; EEG decoding; machine learning; deep learning; explainable AI

1. Introduction
Generative adversarial networks (GAN) and related generative models can now synthesize facial images that preserve many of the visual statistics of natural photographs (Goodfellow et al., 2020; Karras et al., 2019). This creates a difficult perceptual problem: observers may experience a synthetic face as natural without being able to report whether it depicts a real person or an artificial identity. Behavioral studies have shown that highly realistic AI-generated faces are often misclassified as real and may even be judged as more trustworthy or more realistic than genuine human faces (Miller et al., 2023; Nightingale & Farid, 2022). Behavioral accuracy may therefore underestimate the category information represented by the visual and social-cognitive systems.
Electroencephalographic decoding provides a complementary approach because it tests whether distributed neural activity contains information about face origin even when that distinction is irrelevant to the ongoing task. Previous work has shown that neural signals can distinguish realistic AI-generated from real faces (Moshel et al., 2022), and multivariate EEG analyses have identified both relatively early perceptual and later top-down differences in the processing of artificial social stimuli (Tarchi et al., 2023). Earlier ERP research using less realistic artificial faces also reported sustained differences after approximately 400 ms, suggesting that late evaluative processes may remain sensitive to whether a face is perceived as genuinely human (Wheatley et al., 2011).
Face perception unfolds across multiple processing stages. Early occipito-temporal activity reflects structural face encoding, whereas intermediate and late activity contributes to individuation, familiarity, affective evaluation, memory updating, and higher-order social appraisal. Multivariate pattern analysis can detect distributed information that may be weak or absent at any single electrode-time location and is therefore especially useful for high-dimensional EEG data (Grootswagers et al., 2017).
The EEG data analyzed here were originally collected for a companion study that examined conventional ERP amplitudes and swLORETA source reconstructions for AI-generated and real male and female faces (Proverbio & Dosaikina, 2026). That analysis found larger late responses to AI-generated than real faces, including posterior P300 and late positivity and anterior PN400 effects, together with a general right-hemisphere predominance. It also identified origin-by-face-sex interactions, with particularly attenuated responses to real male faces. The present study addresses a distinct question using the same recordings: whether distributed spatiotemporal patterns can decode face origin across participants and which channels and latencies contribute most strongly to classification. Thus, the current work should be understood as a secondary multivariate reanalysis, not as an independent replication of the companion ERP findings.
The present analyses used four experimental conditions: AI-generated male faces (50AM), AI-generated female faces (60AF), real male faces (70RM), and real female faces (80RF). The executed modeling pipelines collapsed these conditions into a binary AI-versus-real contrast, with a chance level of 0.50. We expected the distinction to be weak at individual electrode-time points but more detectable in distributed multivariate patterns. Because the analyzed interval began at 200 ms, we further expected the most informative activity to reflect intermediate or late evaluative processing rather than the canonical N170. All hypotheses were exploratory because the dataset comprised 26 independent participants and only 103 participant-by-condition averages.
2. Related Work and Theoretical Background
The N250 is commonly associated with face individuation, familiarity, and access to stored face representations, although its functional meaning varies with scalp distribution and task (Schweinberger et al., 2004). Later posterior positivities, including the P300 and late positive potential, are associated with attentional allocation, memory updating, perceived relevance, and sustained evaluation (Fields, 2023). Anterior negativities in the N250–N400 range can also be modulated by affective and aesthetic appraisal. In the companion ERP analysis of the present dataset, AI-generated faces elicited enhanced anterior N250/PN400 and posterior P300/late positivity, while real male faces showed the most attenuated responses (Proverbio & Dosaikina, 2026).
Machine-learning models offer complementary advantages. Regularized linear models are often robust in small-sample, high-dimensional settings, whereas nonlinear ensembles can capture interactions among spatial features. Deep architectures can learn joint spatial and temporal filters, but their flexibility increases the risk of overfitting when the number of independent participants is small. Convergence across modeling families is therefore more informative than the nominal superiority of a single algorithm.
A growing body of research has applied machine-learning and deep-learning techniques to the decoding of EEG and event-related potential signals elicited during face perception and visual imagery. Conventional classifiers and time-resolved multivariate pattern analyses can distinguish faces from houses, objects, scenes, and other visual categories, often from single-trial neural activity (Bobrov et al., 2011; Stewart et al., 2014; Cauchoix et al., 2014; Kaneshiro et al., 2015; Mari et al., 2023). Of particular importance, Nemrodov et al. (2018) demonstrated that the distributed spatiotemporal patterns elicited while participants viewed faces contained sufficient information not only to decode individual facial identity but also to reconstruct the visual appearance of the perceived faces, indicating that EEG signals preserve fine-grained, identity-specific representational information rather than merely reflecting generic face detection. Subsequent studies have further decoded face orientation, familiarity, identity, sex, age, and emotional expression from electrophysiological activity (Ambrus et al., 2019; Li et al., 2022; Dalski et al., 2023; Kovács et al., 2023; Mares et al., 2023). Complementary work has employed machine-learning or deep-learning architectures to differentiate neural signals associated with both imagined and perceived stimulus categories (Llorella et al., 2020, 2021; Cudlenco et al., 2020; Zheng et al., 2020; Leoni et al., 2021, 2022, 2023; Bagchi & Bathula, 2022). Specifically, Bobrov et al. (2011) discriminated EEG patterns associated with the visual imagery of human faces and houses—together with a relaxation condition—with classification accuracy reaching 68% in some participants. Llorella et al. (2020), by contrast, used densely connected and convolutional neural networks to classify four imagined objects—a tree, a dog, an airplane, and a house—plus relaxation, achieving an overall success rate of approximately 60%. More recently, Leoni et al. (2023) developed MIRACLE, a hierarchical classification system capable of recognizing ten semantic categories from EEG signals recorded during both stimulus perception and imagination. Collectively, these findings suggest that multivariate and deep-learning approaches can capture distributed category- and identity-specific neural signatures that extend substantially beyond the information conveyed by conventional univariate ERP measurements.
Despite these advances, the application of machine-learning and, particularly, deep-learning methods to the neural discrimination of AI-generated and authentic human faces remains remarkably limited. Moshel et al. (2022) demonstrated that time-resolved EEG patterns could distinguish highly realistic AI-generated faces from real faces using multivariate pattern analysis and a linear discriminant classifier, whereas Beckmann et al. (2024) employed a support-vector classifier to discriminate EEG responses to authentic and manipulated deepfake videos. Related research has also decoded graded levels of face realism from steady-state EEG responses using task-related component analysis rather than learned deep neural architectures (Chen et al., 2024). To the best of our knowledge, however, no previous peer-reviewed study has applied deep learning directly to single-trial ERPs to classify hyper-realistic, fully synthetic GAN-generated faces versus photographs of real individuals. The present study was therefore designed to address this gap by determining whether the artificial origin of perceptually convincing faces can be decoded from their underlying electrophysiological signatures, even when observers are unable to distinguish them reliably at the behavioral level.
3. Methods
3.1 Study design, data provenance, and relation to the companion ERP study
The present study involves an original EEG dataset relative to an ERP and swLORETA investigation by Proverbio and Dosaikina (2026). The original experiment assessed neural responses to real and StyleGAN2-generated faces of both sexes during an orthogonal target-detection task. The companion study analyzed predefined ERP components and source-reconstructed activity. The current study did not repeat those inferential ERP analyses; instead, it used exported participant-by-condition ERP averages to evaluate binary AI-versus-real decoding with linear, classical machine-learning, deep-learning, and explainable-AI methods.
This distinction is important because the current observations are not raw single trials. Each machine-learning observation represents one participant’s average response to one experimental condition. Consequently, the maximum number of statistically independent units is determined primarily by the number of participants rather than by the number of face presentations contributing to each average.
3.2 Participants and ethics
Thirty participants (15 women and 15 men) between 20 and 32 years of age took part in the EEG experiment (mean age = 21.7 years, SD = 2.5). All were right-handed, as assessed with the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory (mean laterality score = 0.73, SD = 0.17), and reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and normal hearing. Participants were recruited through the SONA system and received academic credit. Exclusion criteria included speech or reading disorders; psychiatric, neurological, or neurodevelopmental conditions; a history of epilepsy or substance abuse; and use of psychotropic medication within 48 hours of testing.
All participants provided written informed consent. The study was approved as minimal-risk research by the Ethics Committee of the University of Milano-Bicocca on May 8, 2024 (CRIP Protocol No. RM-2024-822) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Four participants were excluded after ERP averaging because of excessive EEG/EOG artifacts and an insufficient proportion of usable trials. The final EEG sample comprised 26 participants (13 women and 13 men), with a mean age of 21.62 years (SD = 2.45) and a mean laterality score of 0.71 (SD = 0.18).
3.3 Stimuli and independent behavioral validation
The stimulus set comprised 440 standardized full-color face images: 220 real photographs and 220 StyleGAN2-generated faces. Each origin category contained 110 male and 110 female faces. All faces displayed a neutral or positive expression and were selected to approximate the participants’ apparent age range of 18–35 years. To reduce other-race effects, the set included only White faces. Real faces were drawn from the open-access Flickr-Faces-HQ dataset, and synthetic faces were generated using StyleGAN2 (Karras et al., 2019).
Real and AI-generated stimuli were balanced as closely as possible for facial orientation and symmetry, expression, hair color and length, makeup, glasses, earrings or piercings, facial hair, and background type. The images included the neck and upper chest and were matched for luminance across origin categories; differences were small and nonsignificant in the original validation analysis.
The stimuli were independently validated in 50 university students drawn from the same general demographic cohort but not from the EEG sample (Shabani & Proverbio, 2025). One group of 25 participants rated attractiveness and perceived familiarity, whereas a separate group of 25 classified each face as artificial or real. AI-generated faces were rated as more attractive (mean = 3.12 vs. 2.31) and more familiar (mean = 2.70 vs. 2.24) than real faces. Participants identified only 33% of AI-generated faces as artificial, below the 50% chance level, whereas 62% of real faces were correctly classified as real. AI-generated male faces were especially likely to be misclassified as real. Because behavioral and EEG data were collected from independent samples, these findings establish stimulus-level behavioral properties but do not permit within-participant correlations between explicit judgments and neural decoding.
3.4 Experimental procedure
Eight stimulus sequences were programmed in Gentask (Compumedics Neuroscan, Charlotte, NC, USA). Across the experiment, each participant viewed 110 non-target faces from each of the four experimental conditions, for a total of 440 analyzed face presentations before artifact rejection. The four conditions were presented with equal probability and randomly intermixed. Fictitious bluish-tinted faces served as rare targets and were excluded from all ERP and machine-learning analyses.
Participants kept an index finger on the response pad and pressed a button whenever a bluish target appeared. Thus, face origin and face sex were task-irrelevant. Sequence order and response hand were counterbalanced, and sequence order was individually randomized. Each face was displayed for 800 ms, followed by an interstimulus interval jittered between 1900 and 2000 ms. Stimuli subtended approximately 6° × 6° of visual angle (1024 × 1024 pixels; 12 × 12 cm) on a monitor positioned 114 cm from the participant. A small yellow fixation cross remained at the center of the display. Participants completed a structured training session before EEG acquisition. The recording session lasted approximately 45 minutes.
3.5 EEG acquisition and primary ERP preprocessing
Continuous EEG was recorded at 512 Hz using a Compumedics Quick-Cap Neo-Net with 128 sensors arranged according to the 10/5 system (Oostenveld & Praamstra, 2001). Vertical and horizontal electro-oculograms were recorded with bipolar electrodes placed at the outer canthus and above and below the right eye. Signals were referenced to the common average reference.
The original ERP pipeline applied an offline 0.01–70 Hz band-pass filter and a 50 Hz notch filter. Eye blinks were reduced using covariance-based correction, and excessively noisy electrodes were interpolated from four neighboring electrodes. Epochs were time-locked to face onset and extended from −100 to 800 ms. Trials exceeding ±75 µV were rejected, and rare target trials were excluded. Participant-specific ERPs were then averaged separately for AI-generated male, AI-generated female, real male, and real female faces. Each average could contain up to 110 non-target trials before artifact rejection. Participants with excessive residual artifacts and fewer than 30% usable trials were excluded, yielding the final sample of 26 participants.
3.6 Dataset and experimental units for the current reanalysis
The modeling notebook loaded 103 exported participant-by-condition ERP files from the 26 retained participants. The theoretically complete dataset would contain 104 averages (26 participants × 4 conditions), but one condition file was unavailable for participant 01. Condition counts were therefore 25 for 50AM and 26 each for 60AF, 70RM, and 80RF. The first two codes were mapped to AI and the latter two to real, producing 51 AI and 52 real observations for the encoding/decoding and STCNN analyses.
The classical machine-learning benchmark excluded participant 01 to preserve a complete and balanced four-condition structure. This analysis therefore included 25 participants and 100 participant-by-condition averages: 50 AI and 50 real. Throughout this manuscript, these units are termed participant-condition averages or observations, not trials or single-trial epochs.
The four condition codes jointly represented face origin and face sex. Nevertheless, the executed analyses collapsed across face sex. This binary design tests whether face origin can be decoded in the aggregate, but it cannot determine whether performance generalizes across male and female faces. This issue is particularly relevant because the companion ERP analysis identified face-origin × face-sex interactions.
3.7 Machine-learning-specific preprocessing and tensor construction
The secondary pipeline first subtracted a pre-stimulus mean from the numeric EEG channels, excluding time and trigger variables. It selected 14 face-relevant channels: O1, O2, PO9, PO10, TP7, TP8, P3, P4, AF3, AF4, AFF1h, AFF2h, AFF3h, and AFF4h. To retain broader spatial information without using the full 128-channel montage, the remaining channels were grouped by name prefix into frontal, parietal, occipital, and temporal regions. After within-region standardization, the first principal component from each group was retained. The explained-variance ratios were 0.4862 for the frontal, 0.4708 for the parietal, 0.8507 for the occipital, and 0.3708 for the temporal component.
Data were restricted to 200–600 ms after stimulus onset. For each participant-condition average, EEG and PCA features were sorted by time and processed independently. A fourth-order Butterworth low-pass filter at 40 Hz was applied with zero-phase forward-backward filtering, followed by linear detrending. The mean of the first 25 samples in the retained interval was then subtracted. Because these samples are post-stimulus, this operation is more accurately described as local temporal centering than as a conventional prestimulus baseline. Observations longer than 205 samples were truncated, and shorter observations were discarded. An observation was rejected when more than two of the 14 selected EEG channels exceeded an absolute peak of 80 µV.
The resulting tensor had shape (103, 19, 205): 14 selected EEG channels, four regional PCA features, and one auxiliary metadata channel, with 205 time points per observation. The final channel was labelled FaceSEX and was intended to encode the sex of the presented face (female = 1.0, male = 0.0), as derivable from the four experimental condition codes (50AM, 60AF, 70RM, and 80RF). A post hoc audit of the supplied notebook, however, showed that the executed tensor-construction cell populated this channel from SubjectSEX rather than from the target code. Consequently, the current value of this auxiliary channel cannot be interpreted as an estimate of face-sex importance. Before final submission, the STCNN/XAI analysis should be rerun after deriving FaceSEX directly from the condition code. Class labels and participant identifiers were stored separately.
3.8 Encoding and linear decoding
Before modeling, each observation-by-channel time series was z-scored across time. Undefined values resulting from zero temporal variance were replaced with zero. Features with mean temporal variance at or below 1e-6 were removed; because the auxiliary sex feature was constant across time within each observation, it was excluded from the mass-univariate, ridge-encoding, AUC-mapping, and logistic-decoding analyses. Thus, the metadata coding discrepancy did not affect the reported linear analyses, which retained 18 neural/PCA features.
The four stimulus codes were collapsed into binary AI and real labels. At every feature-time location, a one-way ANOVA compared AI and real amplitudes, yielding a mass-univariate F map. The analysis window was then restricted to 220–500 ms, combining a predefined N250 interval (220–290 ms) and a P300/late-positive interval (280–500 ms). This produced 143 time points at approximately 1.95 ms per point. Condition-averaged responses were calculated separately for AI and real observations as descriptive signal checks.
For ridge encoding, binary labels were one-hot encoded into a two-column design matrix. RidgeCV selected the regularization coefficient from 1, 10, 100, 1000, 10,000, 100,000, and 1,000,000 using five-fold cross-validation. The target was the flattened feature-by-time response. Coefficients were reshaped into class-specific spatiotemporal weight maps. Cross-validated R² was also calculated independently for each feature using shuffled five-fold KFold; predictions and targets were stacked across held-out folds before variance-weighted R² was computed.
A complementary AUC map was calculated at every feature-time point from the z-scored amplitudes. Finally, a logistic-regression decoder with cross-validated L2 regularization was evaluated on the flattened 18-feature × 143-time-point representation. Five-fold GroupKFold kept participants separate between training and test sets. The chance level was 0.50.
3.9 Classical machine-learning benchmark
The classical benchmark removed participant 01, discarded the final auxiliary sex-metadata feature, and decimated the 205-point observations by a factor of two, retaining 103 time points. Each time point became an 18-feature spatial sample. The resulting design matrix contained 10,300 rows, while class labels, participant groups, and observation identifiers were repeated across time points. Predictions were aggregated back to the participant-condition level by majority vote; decision scores or probabilities were averaged within each observation. Accordingly, the sex-metadata coding discrepancy did not affect the classical machine-learning results.
Each model was embedded in a leakage-controlled pipeline containing within-participant z-scoring, a tunable scaler, zero-variance removal, ANOVA-F SelectKBest, and the classifier. The scaler search included no additional scaler, MinMaxScaler, and RobustScaler; candidate feature counts were 5, 10, 15, and all features. Eight models were compared: shrinkage linear discriminant analysis, linear support-vector classification, calibrated linear support-vector classification, L1 logistic regression, elastic-net logistic regression, stochastic gradient descent, XGBoost, and a dummy baseline.
The intended full design specified five outer folds repeated five times, five inner splits, and 1000 permutations. The saved output instead used QUICK_TEST = True: two outer StratifiedGroupKFold folds with one repeat, three inner GroupShuffleSplit splits with a 0.20 test fraction, at most three randomized-search iterations per model, and 50 label permutations. Participant groups were disjoint across outer training and test sets. Accuracy, balanced accuracy, macro-F1, Matthews correlation coefficient, and ROC-AUC were computed after aggregation to the participant-condition level. A second focused search was run for the Stage-1 winner and was adopted only if mean accuracy improved.
For the permutation test, a representative XGBoost configuration derived from the focused-search folds was evaluated with group-aware cross-validation. Labels were permuted at the participant-condition level and propagated to all time points belonging to the same observation. The empirical one-sided p value used the plus-one correction. Two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests compared the winner’s two outer-fold accuracies with those of each competing model, followed by Bonferroni correction. Given only two outer folds, these model-comparison tests were treated as descriptive; overlapping training sets can also make conventional cross-validation significance tests anti-conservative (Nadeau & Bengio, 2003).
3.10 SpatialTemporalCNN
The STCNN used the complete (103, 19, 205) tensor and binary labels. Each observation was standardized by its mean and standard deviation across channels and time. Training-time augmentation combined temporal jitter, channel dropout, Gaussian noise, and amplitude scaling. The architecture first applied a learnable dense 19 × 19 spatial mixing matrix normalized with either softmax or sigmoid. One-dimensional temporal convolution, batch normalization, and ELU activation followed. Some fold-specific configurations included a second temporal convolution with a residual projection. Adaptive average pooling reduced the temporal axis to eight values per filter, after which dropout and a fully connected layer produced two class logits.
Evaluation used five outer GroupKFold splits. The saved notebook set RUN_OPTUNA_SEARCH = False and used precomputed fold-specific hyperparameters rather than rerunning optimization. The configurations used 64 or 128 temporal filters, kernel sizes of 4 or 16, one or two convolutional layers, batch sizes of 8 or 16, and 80, 100, or 120 epochs. Models were optimized with Adam and cross-entropy loss. The exact provenance of the precomputed hyperparameters and confirmation that they were selected exclusively within each outer training fold must be documented in the final reproducible analysis package.
3.11 Explainable AI and statistical testing
Channel importance was estimated by permutation occlusion on held-out data. For each of the 19 input features, its observation order was shuffled within each outer test set, and the decrease from the corresponding fold’s baseline accuracy was measured. This procedure was repeated 20 times and averaged across all five folds. Gradient saliency was calculated by differentiating the correct-class logit with respect to each test input, taking the absolute gradient, and averaging across observations and folds.
The saliency map was summarized spatially by mean gradient magnitude per feature and temporally by averaging across features. Temporal saliency was smoothed with a Savitzky-Golay filter using a window length of 11 and a third-order polynomial. XAI results were treated as exploratory. Permutation importance was considered the primary feature-ranking measure because the learned spatial mixing matrix can redistribute information and make raw gradients diffuse or redundant.
4. Results
4.1 Encoding and linear decoding
The binary ridge-encoding model selected α = 100.0 and achieved a training R² of 0.0052. Generalization was poor: the best feature-wise cross-validated R² was −0.0155 at feature index 17. The ANOVA sensitivity map was diffuse, with a maximum F value of 8.57 at feature index 8. No corrected mass-univariate significance test was conducted, so this maximum should not be interpreted as a localized significant effect. The best mean-AUC feature had an AUC of 0.500. The group-aware logistic sanity check performed better than these univariate summaries, reaching 0.613 ± 0.100 accuracy across five folds relative to a binary chance level of 0.50.

4.2 Classical machine learning
All seven trained classifiers met or exceeded the 0.50 dummy accuracy in the reduced run. XGBoost ranked first by mean accuracy at 0.731, whereas linear discriminant analysis produced the highest mean AUC at 0.804. Table 1 reports the exact two-fold means and variances saved in the notebook.

The Stage-2 focused XGBoost search reduced accuracy from 0.731 to 0.659, with macro-F1 = 0.657 and AUC = 0.740, and was therefore not adopted. Using the representative focused-search configuration, the permutation analysis yielded an observed accuracy of 0.710 and an MCC of +0.427. The 50-permutation null distribution had a mean accuracy of 0.470 (variance = 0.0027) and a mean MCC of −0.061 (variance = 0.0109). Both empirical p values were .0196. These values provide preliminary evidence above chance, but the minimum attainable plus-one p value with 50 permutations is coarse, and the reduced configuration is insufficient for publication-quality inference.
No Bonferroni-corrected Wilcoxon comparison was significant. The raw p value was 1.0000 for LDA and 0.5000 for every other comparison; all corrected values were 1.0000. With only two outer folds, these tests cannot reliably rank models.

4.3 SpatialTemporalCNN
The five STCNN outer folds achieved accuracies of 78.3%, 50.0%, 65.0%, 75.0%, and 65.0%, with corresponding AUC values of 0.811, 0.590, 0.860, 0.770, and 0.670. Mean accuracy was 66.7% ± 9.9%, and mean AUC was 0.740. Pooling held-out predictions across folds yielded 69 correct classifications among 103 participant-condition observations. A one-sided binomial test against 0.50 yielded p = .0004. Precision, recall, and F1 were each 0.67 for both classes; macro-F1 and weighted F1 were also 0.67.
Permutation importance emphasized PO10, TP8, O2, and AF4, together with the regional parietal PCA feature. All four named electrodes were right-hemisphere sites, forming a coherent posterior occipito-temporal/temporo-parietal pattern with frontal involvement. The feature plotted as FaceSEX had approximately zero permutation importance. Because the code audit showed that the executed tensor populated this channel from participant rather than face sex, this near-zero value is not interpreted as evidence concerning the contribution of face sex. The substantive XAI pattern was instead carried by EEG and regional PCA features; the STCNN/XAI analysis should nevertheless be rerun with the correctly constructed FaceSEX channel before final submission.
The temporal profile was late rather than early. Saliency peaked near 498 ms and remained elevated across approximately 460–600 ms, spanning a late positive interval rather than the N170. Gradient-based feature saliency was comparatively flat, whereas permutation occlusion produced a clearer ranking. This difference is compatible with redundancy introduced by the learned spatial mixing matrix: a feature may have a substantial local gradient while its information remains recoverable after occlusion, or it may function as a bottleneck that produces a larger accuracy decrease when disrupted.

5. Discussion
The present findings provide preliminary evidence that the artificial origin of highly realistic faces can be decoded from their electrophysiological signatures, even when no single electrode–time-point feature provides a reliable univariate marker. Condition-averaged ERP activity contained a modest binary AI-versus-real signal, but this information was distributed across channels and time points, varied across validation folds, and was not convincingly localized by the linear univariate analyses. Ridge encoding explained virtually none of the response variance, feature-wise cross-validated R² values remained negative, and the mean AUC map did not exceed 0.500. By contrast, participant-grouped multivariate classifiers and the spatiotemporal convolutional neural network (STCNN) performed above the 0.50 baseline. This dissociation suggests that face origin was represented through multivariate interactions among spatially and temporally distributed electrophysiological features rather than through a single dominant ERP effect. This interpretation is consistent with previous EEG-decoding research showing that face information is distributed across patterns that cannot be fully characterized by conventional component-wise analyses. In particular, Nemrodov et al. (2018) demonstrated that EEG patterns contain sufficiently fine-grained information to decode facial identity and reconstruct the appearance of perceived faces. The present findings extend this broader principle from the decoding of identity and other facial attributes to the subtler distinction between photographs of real individuals and hyper-realistic, fully synthetic faces. The logistic decoder reached an accuracy of 0.613 ± 0.100, providing modest evidence for linearly decodable information despite the weak univariate summaries. Within the reduced classical benchmark, XGBoost achieved the highest mean accuracy, whereas shrinkage LDA yielded the highest AUC. These models may have benefited from different inductive biases. Shrinkage LDA is well suited to small-sample, high-dimensional data because it imposes a strongly regularized linear decision boundary, whereas XGBoost can capture nonlinear interactions among spatial and temporally summarized features. Nevertheless, the focused XGBoost search reduced rather than improved performance, indicating that hyperparameter selection was unstable in the present sample. The results therefore do not justify declaring any individual algorithm intrinsically superior.
The STCNN findings were broadly compatible with those of the classical benchmark. Its mean accuracy of 66.7% and AUC of 0.740 did not exceed the leading reduced-run classical models, which is unsurprising given the imbalance between the flexibility of a deep neural architecture and the availability of only 26 independent participants. Accordingly, the primary contribution of the STCNN was not a decisive improvement in predictive performance, but its capacity to model joint spatial–temporal dependencies and provide an integrated attribution profile. Permutation importance identified a predominantly right-sided posterior configuration with additional frontal involvement, including O2, PO10, TP8, the PCA_Parietal feature, and AF4. The concentration of informative named electrodes at even-numbered sites, conventionally corresponding to the right hemisphere in the 10/5 montage, is consistent with the established right lateralization of face processing. This anatomical coherence increases confidence that the model exploited a face-relevant electrophysiological configuration rather than an entirely arbitrary subset of sensors, although it remains insufficient for cortical localization.
5.1 Relation to previous EEG decoding studies of face realness
The present findings should be interpreted in relation to the small but relevant literature applying multivariate EEG analyses to the perception of artificial faces. The closest precedent is the study by Moshel et al. (2022), who used time-resolved multivariate pattern classification to distinguish realistic AI-generated faces from photographs of real individuals. Their results showed that neural responses contained information about face origin even when behavioral discrimination was limited, thereby establishing an important dissociation between conscious judgments and decodable neural representations. The above-chance performance observed in the present study converges with this central finding and further indicates that origin-related information can be recovered from condition-averaged ERP representations using both classical classifiers and a deep spatiotemporal architecture.
The two studies nevertheless differ in several theoretically and methodologically relevant respects. Moshel et al. (2022) primarily examined the temporal emergence of decodable real-versus-artificial information using time-resolved linear multivariate analyses. The present study instead compared multiple modeling approaches, including regularized linear classification, nonlinear tree-based methods, and an STCNN, while using participant-grouped validation to evaluate generalization to individuals excluded from model training. It additionally combined predictive modeling with spatial–temporal attribution to identify the sensor configuration and latency range contributing most strongly to classification. Chen et al. (2024) provided complementary evidence that perceived face realness can be decoded from EEG, but their study addressed a different representational problem. Participants viewed periodically presented faces spanning a continuum from highly stylized cartoons to photographs, and realness levels were classified from steady-state visual evoked potentials using task-related component analysis. Their findings demonstrated a nonlinear neural response to progressive stylization and emphasized the importance of controlling low-level properties, including eye size. However, the distinction between graded stylization levels is not equivalent to the present binary contrast between hyper-realistic, GAN-generated faces and authentic photographs, and SSVEP classification does not directly address the transient ERP dynamics examined here. Beckmann et al. (2024) also investigated EEG-informed deepfake detection using a support-vector classifier to distinguish authentic from digitally manipulated facial stimuli. That work represents an important proof of concept for incorporating neural features into deepfake-detection systems, but it was based on a single-participant dataset, concerned manipulated rather than entirely synthetic identities, and was reported as a conference contribution and preprint. In contrast, the present analysis concerned fully synthetic GAN-generated faces, evaluated generalization across 26 independent participants, and incorporated a convolutional architecture that learned directly from the spatial–temporal organization of the electrophysiological data.
Thus, the present study is not the first demonstration that EEG contains information distinguishing real from artificial facial stimuli. Its specific contribution lies in evaluating whether condition-averaged transient ERP patterns elicited by hyper-realistic, fully synthetic identities can be classified using both conventional machine-learning algorithms and a deep spatiotemporal neural network. To the best of our knowledge, previous peer-reviewed work had not applied a spatiotemporal deep-learning architecture directly to this specific ERP classification problem. The findings therefore extend earlier linear decoding results while providing a preliminary assessment of the potential—and current limitations—of deep learning for detecting the neural signatures of synthetic face perception.
5.2 Relation to the conventional ERP findings from the same dataset
The late, right-lateralized attribution pattern is broadly consistent with the conventional ERP results obtained from the same recordings, although it should not be regarded as an independent replication. The companion ERP analysis found larger P300 and late-positive activity over the right than the left hemisphere, together with significant AI-versus-real differences at occipital and occipito-temporal sites, including O1/O2 and PO9/PO10 (Proverbio & Dosaikina, 2026). O2 is readily interpretable as an index of posterior visual processing. PO10 is particularly informative because, despite its “PO” designation, its lateral scalp position more closely approximates the right occipito-temporal face-processing region than a generic parietal site. Its prominence is compatible with evidence that the fusiform gyrus and functionally defined fusiform face area make major contributions to face-sensitive electrophysiological activity (Gao et al., 2019), and that the right fusiform system plays a central role in facial-identity recognition (Rossion et al., 2024). PO10 must not, however, be equated with the fusiform face area itself, because activity recorded at a scalp electrode reflects mixtures of multiple cortical generators. TP8 and AF4 extend this posterior visual configuration toward regions potentially involved in higher-order social evaluation. TP8 overlies the right lateral temporo-parietal scalp, in proximity to regions implicated in representing other individuals’ beliefs and mental states (Saxe & Wexler, 2005). AF4 samples right anterior frontal activity and may reflect frontal contributions to implicit social appraisal and mentalizing. Such an interpretation is conceptually compatible with the orthogonal task used here, because participants were not explicitly asked to determine whether a face was real, artificial, humanlike, or endowed with a mind (Kivity et al., 2024). Nevertheless, the importance assigned to TP8 and AF4 cannot establish that participants explicitly attributed mental states to the faces, nor can it demonstrate anthropomorphism in the absence of corresponding behavioral measures or source-localized activity. Their contribution is more conservatively described as compatible with a late face-related and social-evaluative network.
The temporal profile further constrains this interpretation. Because the analyzed interval began at 200 ms and model saliency peaked at approximately 498 ms, classification appeared to rely more strongly on intermediate and late evaluative activity than on the early structural encoding conventionally indexed by the N170. This timing is compatible with sustained appraisal, familiarity-related processing, and potentially implicit evaluation of the social agency conveyed by a face. It also accords with evidence that electrophysiological differences between real and artificial faces can persist beyond 400 ms (Wheatley et al., 2011). Taken together, the right occipito-temporal, temporo-parietal, and anterior pattern suggests a plausible progression from perceptual face analysis to higher-order evaluation. Because the experiment did not collect trial-wise authenticity judgments, measures of mind perception, or anthropomorphism ratings, however, this configuration should be characterized as a late face-related evaluative network rather than as definitive evidence of mentalizing.
5.3 Face sex as a potential moderator of binary decoding
The companion ERP study showed that the effects of face origin were not uniform across male and female faces. Real male faces elicited particularly attenuated N250, P300, PN400, and late-positive responses, whereas the responses to AI-generated male and female faces were more similar. Because the present models collapsed the four experimental conditions into a binary AI-versus-real contrast, classification performance may partly reflect this disproportionately distinctive response to real male faces rather than an origin-related signal that generalizes equally across face sex. The auxiliary channel labelled FaceSEX was intended to determine whether explicit information about the sex of the presented face could provide a shortcut for classification. However, because the supplied tensor was inadvertently populated with participant sex, its near-zero importance cannot answer that question. This coding error does not affect the linear or classical machine-learning analyses, from which the channel was excluded, but it requires correction and rerunning of the STCNN and explainable-AI pipeline.  Independently of this metadata channel, information about face sex may already be embedded in both the EEG responses and the visual properties of the four stimulus conditions. A more stringent test of origin-specific neural information should therefore include separate AI-versus-real decoders for male and female faces, a factorial model incorporating origin and face sex, and cross-sex generalization analyses in which a model trained on male faces is evaluated on female faces and vice versa. Successful cross-sex transfer would provide stronger evidence that the classifier captures neural information related to artificial origin rather than sex-specific stimulus differences.
5.4 Interpretation, limitations, and evidential strength
The strongest aspect of the present evidence is its convergence across methods rather than the performance of any single model: modest participant-grouped logistic decoding, preliminary above-chance classical machine-learning performance, balanced STCNN class metrics, and a late right-hemisphere attribution pattern overlapping with the sensor-level ERP effects obtained from the same recordings. None of these findings is decisive in isolation.
Several limitations constrain the conclusions. First, condition averaging increased the signal-to-noise ratio but substantially reduced the number of observations and prevented assessment of trial-to-trial variability. Consequently, the present analysis cannot establish whether individual AI-generated faces could be classified from single-trial responses. Second, although participant-grouped validation tested generalization to unseen individuals, it did not provide evidence for generalization to unfamiliar image datasets, novel generative models, or faces produced using different synthesis techniques. This distinction is particularly important for synthetic-face detection, because classifiers can exploit properties specific to a particular generator or stimulus set rather than a general neural representation of artificiality.
Third, the analysis cannot completely exclude the contribution of systematic low-level differences between the AI-generated and real-face sets. Variations in spatial frequency, contrast, texture, skin homogeneity, eye appearance, lighting, or photographic noise could contribute to the electrophysiological distinction. Chen et al. (2024) demonstrated the importance of explicitly evaluating such stimulus-level confounders when decoding face realness. Future studies should therefore quantify and match low-level image statistics, include faces generated by multiple GAN and diffusion architectures, incorporate photographs acquired under heterogeneous conditions, and evaluate cross-generator and cross-dataset transfer. Training a model on one generative source and testing it on another would provide a substantially stronger test of whether the decoded signal reflects artificial origin rather than generator-specific visual artifacts. Fourth, permutation importance and saliency identify model-relevant inputs but do not establish the cortical generators or cognitive operations responsible for classification. The attribution results should therefore be interpreted as descriptive evidence concerning sensors and temporal intervals, not as a substitute for source reconstruction, causal inference, or independently measured psychological processes. Finally, the small number of independent participants limits the effective complexity that can be supported by the data and probably explains why the STCNN did not outperform the simpler classical algorithms.
Accordingly, the present study should be framed as a proof of concept for multivariate and deep-learning reanalysis of a previously collected ERP dataset. It demonstrates that highly realistic AI-generated and authentic faces elicit electrophysiological patterns that are modestly but systematically distinguishable at the group-generalization level, even when conventional univariate feature maps provide little evidence for a dominant local marker. Replication using larger samples, single-trial decoding, nested validation, multiple stimulus generators, and independent external datasets will be required before such neural signatures can be considered robust, generalizable markers of synthetic-face perception.The analyses support a qualified conclusion: condition-averaged ERP activity contained a preliminary binary AI-versus-real signal, but that signal was distributed, variable across folds, and not convincingly localized by the linear univariate analyses. Ridge encoding explained almost none of the response variance, feature-wise cross-validated R² values remained negative, and the mean AUC map did not exceed 0.500. In contrast, group-aware multivariate classifiers and the STCNN exceeded the 0.50 baseline. This pattern is consistent with the hypothesis that the distinction depends on interactions among channels and time points rather than on one dominant electrode-time feature.
The logistic decoder reached 0.613 ± 0.100 accuracy, providing modest linear decoding evidence despite weak univariate summaries. In the reduced classical benchmark, XGBoost achieved the highest mean accuracy, whereas LDA achieved the highest AUC. These models likely benefited from different inductive biases. Shrinkage LDA is well suited to a small-sample regime because it imposes a strongly regularized linear boundary, whereas XGBoost can capture nonlinear interactions among spatial features. However, the focused XGBoost search reduced rather than improved performance, indicating unstable hyperparameter selection. The present results do not justify declaring one model intrinsically superior. The STCNN result was broadly compatible with the classical benchmark. Its mean accuracy of 66.7% and AUC of 0.740 did not exceed the leading reduced-run classical models, which is unsurprising given the ratio between model flexibility and only 26 independent participants. Its principal added value was the joint spatial-temporal attribution. Permutation importance identified a posterior right-hemisphere cluster with frontal involvement: O2, PO10, TP8, the PCA_Parietal feature, and AF4. The concentration of informative named electrodes at even-numbered sites, which index the right hemisphere in the 10/5 montage, is strikingly consistent with the established right lateralization of face processing. This anatomical coherence increases confidence that the classifier relied on a face-relevant neural configuration rather than on an arbitrary set of sensors, while remaining insufficient for cortical localization.
6. Limitations
The primary limitation is the number of independent participants. Although each condition average was derived from up to 110 face presentations, the tensor contained only 103 participant-condition averages from 26 participants, and the classical benchmark contained 100 averages from 25 participants. Fold estimates were consequently unstable: STCNN accuracy ranged from 50.0% to 78.3%, and the reduced classical benchmark provided only two outer-fold values per model.
Second, the classical-ML results are explicitly preliminary. QUICK_TEST = True reduced the intended analysis to two outer folds, one repeat, three inner splits, three randomized-search iterations, and 50 permutations. The permutation p value of .0196 is therefore coarse, and the Wilcoxon comparisons are effectively uninformative. The full repeated outer evaluation and at least 1000 group-preserving permutations are required before confirmatory claims are made.
Third, the pooled STCNN binomial test treated 103 held-out participant-condition predictions as independent Bernoulli observations, although multiple observations came from each participant. This can make significance anti-conservative. Confirmatory inference should aggregate predictions at the participant level, use hierarchical models, or implement participant-wise permutation procedures. Cross-validation comparisons also inherit dependence from overlapping training sets (Nadeau & Bengio, 2003).
Fourth, the behavioral validation and EEG data came from independent samples. The study therefore shows neural decodability of a stimulus category that was difficult for a demographically similar behavioral sample, but it cannot demonstrate a within-person neural-behavioral dissociation. A future experiment should collect authenticity judgments and confidence ratings from the same participants, preferably in a separate session that preserves naivety during EEG acquisition.
Fifth, the current analysis used condition averages rather than raw single trials. Trial-level data would support more independent training examples, estimation of within-participant uncertainty, temporal generalization analyses, and direct comparison between neural confidence and behavioral responses. Exact retained trial counts for each exported average should also be preserved and reported in the analysis archive.
Sixth, binary collapsing may confound face origin with the origin-by-face-sex interaction identified in the companion ERP study. Within-sex decoding and cross-sex generalization are necessary before the signal can be described as invariant to face sex.
Seventh, the machine-learning pipeline applied local centering using the first 25 samples after restricting the data to 200–600 ms. Because this is a post-stimulus interval, robustness should be tested with alternative preprocessing strategies, including use of the original prestimulus baseline only. Regional PCA and every data-dependent transformation should also be estimated exclusively within the training folds in the confirmatory pipeline.
Finally, the mass-univariate F map lacked a multiple-comparison correction, the mean AUC map was not a strong localization statistic, and scalp-feature importance cannot identify cortical generators. The functional interpretations of O2, PO10, TP8, and AF4 are therefore anatomically and neuroscientifically plausible but remain inferential. In particular, PO10 should be described as a right occipito-temporal scalp site compatible with fusiform face-network activity, not as a direct recording of the FFA; likewise, TP8 and AF4 cannot by themselves prove mentalizing or anthropomorphism. The auxiliary FaceSEX channel must be reconstructed from the target condition and the STCNN/XAI analysis rerun. The provenance of the precomputed STCNN hyperparameters must also be fully documented to demonstrate that outer test data did not influence model selection.
7. Conclusion and Future Work
This secondary analysis provides preliminary evidence that condition-averaged ERPs can distinguish AI-generated from real faces when face origin is irrelevant to the task. Linear encoding was weak, whereas multivariate classical models and the STCNN produced above-chance binary decoding. The interpretable deep model emphasized a late, strongly right-lateralized pattern involving O2, PO10, TP8, the parietal PCA feature, and AF4, with temporal saliency peaking near 498 ms. The spatial distribution is neuroscientifically coherent with a progression from posterior visual and occipito-temporal face processing toward temporo-parietal and frontal social evaluation. It is also broadly consistent with late right-sided ERP effects previously observed in the same dataset and is more compatible with sustained face evaluation than with an early N170-driven visual difference. These findings remain preliminary until they are replicated at the single-trial level, evaluated with a full nested-validation design, tested separately across face sex, and confirmed after correction of the auxiliary FaceSEX channel.
A confirmatory study should retain raw single-trial EEG, prespecify participant-level inference, run the full repeated nested cross-validation and permutation scheme, and evaluate origin within each face-sex category. Cross-sex generalization would provide a particularly stringent test of whether the classifier detects artificial origin rather than sex-specific stimulus structure. Collecting explicit authenticity judgments and confidence from the same participants would clarify whether neural decoding exceeds individual behavioral discrimination. External validation on faces generated by different models and on an independently acquired EEG sample will be necessary to establish generalizability.
